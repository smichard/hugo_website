I first came across Bill Gurley in March 2026, on the Prof G Markets episode "The AI Divide: Who Wins and Who Gets Replaced," where he talked about his new book. I added it to my reading list and left it there. Some weeks later a teammate pointed me to his [article](https://p3institute.substack.com/p/from-open-source-software-to-open) from May 2026. It argues that pursuing an open-source development model can be a competitive strategy in itself, and that a company running an leveraging such a model well can turn it into a lasting advantage over its competitors. It is a long read, and Gurley works through case after case from the last twenty years of the industry before applying the same lens to questions that are still open, including the direction AI models take. Coming across his name twice inside a few weeks was enough to move the book up my list.

## The Author

**Bill Gurley** was a General Partner at Benchmark Capital from 1999 until he stepped back from active investing in 2020. His board seats have included Uber, Zillow, OpenTable, Stitch Fix, and Nextdoor. Outside of investing, he used to write on his blog [Above the Crowd](https://abovethecrowd.com/) and, more recently, on his [Substack](https://p3institute.substack.com/), where the open-source strategy piece appeared. *Runnin' Down a Dream: How to Thrive in a Career You Actually Love* is his first book, published in February 2026.

## What the Book Covers

The book grew out of a talk Gurley gave to MBA students at the University of Texas McCombs School of Business in September 2018, which found a far wider audience once it went online. That talk laid out five principles, illustrated through profiles of Bobby Knight, Bob Dylan, Danny Meyer, Sam Hinkie, and Katrina Lake. The book has six: chase your curiosity, hone your craft, develop mentors, embrace your peers, go to where the action is, and give back.

Going to where the action is was not in the talk. The more interesting change is the first principle. In the talk it was find your passion. In the book it is chase your curiosity, a shift Gurley traces back to what he learned from Angela Duckworth's research on passion and perseverance while working on the manuscript.

The research behind all this took about six years: biographies, the academic literature on job satisfaction and career regret, and conversations with people like Duckworth, Adam Grant, and Daniel Pink. The pattern Gurley kept finding was that people who thrive in their careers often start at the bottom of a field their parents would have talked them out of, then stay curious about it long after curiosity stops being required of them. The case studies carry the argument (Danny Meyer, Sam Hinkie, and talent agent Lorrie Bartlett among them), which keeps the six principles from reading as a list of rules.

## My Take

The core argument is not a hard sell for me: staying curious about your field long after you are competent in it is what separates a career from a job. I try to keep up with what is happening in my own industry the same way. What stuck with me is that Gurley treats curiosity as a habit you build and maintain. That is a more useful framing than curiosity as a personality trait you either have or do not.

It also brought back a couple of things from my time at EMC. One of the executives I worked under had a catchphrase he repeated often: "know your brief," meaning know your subject well enough that nobody can catch you flat-footed on it. A lot of the Presales organization at EMC also worked by Chad Sakac's Presales manifesto, which was built around the same instincts Gurley writes about: keep learning constantly, and apply something like the 80/20 rule to where you spend your energy. Reading Gurley's chapter on curiosity felt like reading a more academic, more research-backed version of ideas I had already absorbed secondhand almost a decade earlier.

I would still not call this a book full of new ideas. Stay curious, find mentors, be where the interesting work is happening, give back once you can: most people have heard all of that in some form before. Gurley's contribution is the packaging. The case studies are well chosen, and the research behind the curiosity chapter is solid. The middle chapters drag, though, and 272 pages is more than these six ideas need.

For someone early in their career, still figuring out what to specialize in or how to think about the next ten years, this is a reasonable book to pick up. It will not change how someone with fifteen years of experience thinks about their own career, but it is not trying to. I am glad I read it, but I would not recommend it to everyone.
